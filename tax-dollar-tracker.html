What this covers. Federal and provincial income tax only. It does not include CPP or EI contributions, HST, property tax, or fuel and carbon charges — so it is not the full gap between your gross pay and what lands in your account. The effective rate is tax as a share of all your income; the marginal rate applies only to your next dollar earned. The marginal rate is always the higher of the two, and it is the one people usually have in mind when they say what bracket they are in.

Sources & Notes

  • Tax brackets (2025): Canada Revenue Agency / TaxTips.ca — Federal and provincial/territorial rates
  • Federal spending: Receiver General for Canada — Public Accounts of Canada 2025, Volume I, Section 3 (Expenses), fiscal year 2024–25 actuals. These are audited actuals, not budget projections. The denominator is total expenses excluding net actuarial losses, $543,279M; net actuarial losses of $4,020M are a non-cash revaluation of pension and future-benefit obligations and are left out of both the categories and the total.

    Largest line items: elderly benefits $80,294M (Old Age Security $60,648M, Guaranteed Income Supplement $18,910M, Allowance $632M), public debt charges $53,410M, Employment Insurance $24,880M plus the Canada Child Benefit $28,574M, the Canada Health Transfer $52,070M, Equalization $25,253M with Territorial Financing $5,159M, and the Canada Social Transfer $16,909M.

    Transfers and debt are taken as program lines; the functional categories are ministry totals, net of each ministry's own public debt charges. No ministry is counted on both sides, so the two cuts do not overlap. Other is a residual — the total less every named category — and it holds Canadian Heritage, Energy and Natural Resources, Fisheries and Oceans, Global Affairs' diplomatic and consular operations, the operating costs of the Finance, Employment and Workforce Development and National Revenue ministries, and the negative provision for valuation and other items.

    Two rows need a caveat. Veterans Affairs is the only category not on the ministry basis: the Public Accounts show the Veterans Affairs segment at $546M because veterans' disability and future benefits are recognised as personnel expenses government-wide, so the figure used is total expenses from Veterans Affairs' audited departmental financial statements ($7,209M). Carbon Price Rebates covers the Climate action incentive and the portion of fuel charge proceeds returned to small business. The consumer fuel charge ended during 2025, so this row falls away in later years — it is shown because it was real spending in 2024–25.
  • Provincial spending: Statistics Canada — Table 10-10-0005-01, Canadian Classification of Functions of Government (CCOFOG) by consolidated government component, reference year 2024, released 27 November 2025. The series used is consolidated provincial-territorial and local governments. Each category is a CCOFOG function or a group of them, so the ten shares are a partition of total expenditure and sum to 100% without adjustment.

    Two things follow from that choice and are worth stating plainly. The universe is wider than the provincial government. It consolidates municipalities, school boards, hospitals and universities along with the province, which is why the column is labelled provincial, territorial and local rather than provincial. Statistics Canada publishes this consolidated series precisely because provinces delegate different functions to municipalities, so it is the basis on which jurisdictions can be compared; the province-only series carries a footnote advising against comparison. It is an operating-expense view. CCOFOG excludes capital acquisition and consumption of fixed capital, so long-lived assets are understated — most visibly for transportation, where roads and transit are capital-heavy.
  • How a tax dollar is allocated: your federal tax is spread across federal spending in proportion to what Ottawa spends on each category, and your provincial tax across provincial-territorial-local spending the same way. This assumes personal income tax funds every category in the same proportion as total spending, which is a simplification: income tax is roughly half of federal revenue and about a quarter of provincial revenue, and no revenue source is earmarked to a particular category. It is the same method the UK uses for its Annual Tax Summary.

    Federal transfers appear on both sides. The Canada Health Transfer, Canada Social Transfer, Equalization and Territorial Formula Financing are shown as federal rows, because that is where your federal tax goes. Your province then spends those same dollars, so they are also inside the provincial column — the CHT within health, the CST across social services and post-secondary education, Equalization and TFF across everything. The two columns answer different questions and the overlap is not netted out.
  • Spending shares are rounded to one decimal place. Actual allocations vary year to year.
  • Ontario: the surtax (20% of Ontario tax over $5,710, plus a further 36% over $7,307) and the Ontario Health Premium (up to $900, collected as Ontario income tax on Form ON428) are both applied. The Ontario Tax Reduction, which lowers or eliminates provincial tax for low-income filers and those with dependants, is not — so Ontario tax is overstated at the bottom of the income range.
  • The Quebec federal abatement (16.5%) is applied. Ontario is the only province or territory with an income tax surtax in 2025; PEI's was eliminated for 2024 and later and folded into its bracket rates. See the methodology document for the full list of simplifications.
